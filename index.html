The constraint that forced this whole plan

The Orbi 970 series cannot tag VLANs in AP mode — Netgear disables VLAN support entirely in that mode. Its guest network is worse than nothing: it hands out 192.168.2.x but NATs everything behind the Orbi's own LAN IP, so guest traffic is invisible to the Firewalla and guest clients retain application-layer reach into the LAN. Firewalla's own answer is "use a different access point." Every wireless device you own is stuck in one L2 domain until the Orbi is retired.

Upstream pressure

In 24 hours, Nest-Cam pushed 11.4 GB up and Nest-5B38 another 6.8 GB; googleapis.com absorbed 20.7 GB of your 30 GB total daily upload. Against Spectrum's hard 35 Mb/s upstream ceiling that is a large, permanent tax on every other outbound flow. Moving to locally-recorded UniFi Protect drops it to near zero — the strongest single argument for the camera swap.

2 · Target architecture

Two principles shape this. First, the Gold Pro stays the single edge — it does 10 Gbps with full IDS/IPS running, three regions blocked and a 1,000-entry target list, so it is not the bottleneck and doesn't need help. Second, lab east-west traffic never crosses a Firewalla; the rack gets its own top-of-rack switch and the second Gold only handles the lab's north-south path.

5 · Wi-Fi design

Decided 22 Aug: start with two APs (~930 sq ft each) and pull cable for a conditional third — see docs/04. Even two APs in this footprint is dense spacing. It will perform well, but only with deliberate tuning; defaults will produce the radios fighting each other.

BandChannel widthPlan
6 GHz320 MHzOne per AP. US 6 GHz has three non-overlapping 320 MHz channels in Low Power Indoor (LPi) mode — which is what indoor consumer APs use, so it applies to you. (In Standard Power mode the US allows only one 320 MHz channel.) Two or three APs fit with zero co-channel. Note the asymmetry: the AP7 Desktop is 4×4 on 6 GHz (11.5 Gbps); the Ceiling units are only 2×2 (5.76 Gbps). Your central AP7D is the strongest radio in the house — put your most demanding clients near it.
5 GHz40 MHzNot 80. At this density 80 MHz forces channel reuse and airtime contention. 40 MHz gives enough non-overlapping channels to keep all three clean.
2.4 GHz20 MHzDisable on at least one AP, and cut TX power hard on the other two. Kasa devices are 2.4-only and cling to first association — you want this coverage deliberate and minimal, not blanketing.

SSID → VLAN mapping

  • Main → Trusted (VLAN 20) — 6 GHz + 5 GHz, WPA3
  • IoT → IoT (VLAN 30) — 2.4 GHz only, WPA2 (most Kasa gear won't do WPA3)
  • Media → Media (VLAN 50) — 5 GHz, for anything not hardwired
  • Guest → Guest (VLAN 60) — client isolation on, captive portal optional
Vaulted ceilings

Firewalla specs AP7C mounting at 6–10 ft. A vault peak at 14–18 ft gives you a weak, splayed, downward pattern precisely where clients aren't. Wall-mount on the vault wall at 8–10 ft, or use flat ceiling sections. The AP7D wants 3–5 ft off the floor in a central spot — confirm you have both an outlet and a drop there before ordering it.

Uplinks

Each AP7C has a 10G PoE port and a 2.5G port, both bridged. Run the 10G port to Switch X as a tagged trunk carrying all Wi-Fi VLANs, with an untagged native VLAN for AP management — Firewalla's guide is explicit that AP and box ports are trunk ports and need a native untagged VLAN for control traffic. Note also that Wi-Fi networks can only use VLANs present on the same port set as the management LAN.

  • Operation Mode: AP, DHCP off, gateway 192.168.187.1 — correctly configured behind the Firewalla.
  • Guest network is OFF (SSID NETGEAR-Guest, Wireless AP: Off). The AP-mode guest hole is not live on your network. That closes the security item.

Why your 5 GHz choices are so limited — and why it can't be fixed

The Orbi 970 is quad-band: 2.4 GHz, two 5 GHz bands, and 6 GHz. The upper 5 GHz band (5GHz-1) is permanently dedicated to wireless backhaul and is never available to clients — and that reservation persists even when backhaul is wired. That is the explanation for something that looked like a firmware bug earlier: the 5 GHz channel list offers only 36–64, with no UNII-3 (149–165), because those upper channels are reserved for a backhaul your satellites aren't using. You are giving up an entire band for nothing, permanently, with no setting to reclaim it.

This also revises the MLO advice. Netgear builds the backhaul by combining 5GHz-1 and 6 GHz into a single MLO link, so turning off 240 MHz would not free the reserved 5 GHz band — that stays gone regardless — and any 6 GHz gain is speculative while backhaul is wired. Meanwhile the cost is real: we watched Satellite-MB fall back to wireless backhaul during the channel change, so that path does get used, and narrowing it makes the fallback worse. Net: leave 240 MHz enabled.

Add this to the AP7 case. Three Orbi units in 1,860 sq ft, no transmit power control, and a whole 5 GHz band burned on an unused backhaul.

Management plane hygiene

The admin UI is HTTP Basic Auth over plain HTTP — the password crosses the wire base64-encoded on a flat network shared with 116 devices. The Wi-Fi PSK is also displayed in cleartext on the Basic page. Both problems disappear once management lives on VLAN 10 behind an AP platform the Firewalla actually manages.

Correction: an earlier draft of this document claimed the RBE971's admin daemon had failed. It had not. The admin UI was unreachable because the client browser sat behind a Netgear Nighthawk 5G router, which intercepted admin-login traffic bound for the Orbi. Connecting from a client not behind the Nighthawk resolved it immediately. The RBE971 was healthy throughout.

Carry these to the AP7 build

  • Main SSID: pinnacle → Trusted (VLAN 20), security mode "Mixed Personal". Resolved: the AP7 supports per-band security overriding under exactly this name — Mixed Personal "uses WPA2 for 2.4/5 GHz, and WPA3 for 6 GHz" on one SSID. Same SSID, same PSK, legacy devices keep joining on 2.4/5, modern clients get 6 GHz. Do not pick the AP7's "WPA2/WPA3" mode — on the AP7, that mode does not enable 6 GHz at all. Two caveats: 6 GHz is disabled on SSIDs carrying microsegmentation personal keys (PPSK is WPA2-only), and some legacy IoT can only join WPA2-only SSIDs — which is what pinnacle-IoT remains for.
  • IoT SSID: pinnacle-IoT — 2.4 GHz only, WPA2-PSK → map to IoT (VLAN 30); keep the same PSK and the ~28 Kasa devices re-join without re-provisioning
  • AP locations: the two satellite positions (MB and G) already have wired drops — those are your AP7C mounts. The AP7D goes in the RBE971's current central spot.

Cat6 validates the Switch X purchase

Cat6 carries 10GBASE-T to 55 m (180 ft) per TIA — versus 100 m for Cat6A. One honest caveat: that 55 m assumes reasonable conditions, and tightly bundled cable in conduit is exactly the alien crosstalk scenario that pushes the practical figure toward 37 m. In an 1,860 sq ft single-story home, runs from a central closet are realistically 15–25 m, so you are inside even the conservative number. Switch X's 10G copper is genuinely usable. Had this been Cat5e the recommendation would have been two Switch SEs instead — it isn't, so buy the X.

The APs need new ceiling runs — existing drops can't serve them

All six live drops terminate at wall plates near the floor. An AP7C is a ceiling mount, and section 5 already concluded it wants 8–10 ft of height on a vault wall. You cannot feed a ceiling-mounted AP from a baseboard jack without pulling new cable — so 2 new attic runs are required for the ceiling APs regardless of the camera project. Attic access makes this straightforward. The AP7D is the exception: it's a desktop unit, so it can live on furniture off an existing room drop.

Cabling scope and the port ceiling

New runs needed: 2 for ceiling APs (Cat6A — 10G headroom) and ~8 for exterior cameras (Cat6 is ample; cameras need 1G at most). Exterior runs under the eaves of a single-story house with attic access are among the easiest cabling work there is.

Closet runs terminate on three Legrand AC106A6 modules inside the On-Q (18 Cat6A positions, owned); camera runs go to the garage. No 19" patch panel anywhere.

Recommended pull list

Attic access reaches a false ceiling throughout, so runs are cheap and repeatable. Pull generously now; buy switches later. Cable is the thing you can't easily redo once the ceiling is closed and the gear is live — a switch is a purchase you can defer to the week you actually fill the ports. This is the one place in the project where over-building is the frugal choice.

11 · Publishing websites — the pfSense question

The goal: several websites on separate domains, hosted on different internal machines, DNS in Cloudflare, reachable from the internet through one public IP. pfSense was bought to solve this.

Why a proxy is structurally required here

NAT operates at layer 3/4 and cannot see a hostname. A port forward for 443 points at exactly one internal host, forever. The domain name only appears inside the TLS handshake (SNI) and the HTTP Host header — both above the layer NAT works at. So splitting many domains across one public IP requires something that terminates or inspects at layer 7. There is no firewall configuration that substitutes for it.

Corollary: every domain's A record must point at your single public IP (or CNAME to a tunnel). The proxy can only route traffic that actually reaches it.

The placement is the problem, not the software

pfSense currently sits at 192.168.187.99 / .185 — on the flat LAN, in the same broadcast domain as your Macs, the UNAS-Pro, the cameras and 28 cloud-connected smart plugs. A reverse proxy is by definition the one host you intend the internet to reach. Putting it on the same L2 segment as everything you care about means a compromise of the one thing designed to be attacked lands it directly beside the things that must not be. This is the single strongest argument in this whole document for segmentation, and it applies whichever proxy software you pick.

Three ways to do it

You're already using Cloudflare DNS, so this is the natural fit — and it's free. Hostname-to-backend routing is configured in Cloudflare rather than locally.
Reverse proxy + port forward

Chosen build: pfSense single-armed on the Silicom box

Single-armed resolves the redundancy objection

An earlier draft argued pfSense would add a second routing and NAT layer behind a Gold Pro that already does the job. That is true only if pfSense is a gateway. Deployed single-armed — one interface on the DMZ, default route pointing at the Firewalla, routing nothing for anyone — it is simply a host that happens to run HAProxy. No second NAT, no chained port forwards, and you keep both the hardware and an OS you already know.

Port 443 collision — the gotcha that will cost you an evening

pfSense's own web GUI binds 443 by default, which is exactly the port HAProxy needs. Move the GUI first — System → Advanced → TCP Port, set 8443 — before configuring the HAProxy frontend. Netgate's own documentation says moving the GUI "will free up the standard web ports for use with port forwards or other services such as HAProxy." They also warn: "Do not expose the GUI to untrusted networks such as the Internet even on non-default ports" — port obscurity is not security. That is precisely why NIC 2 sits on the management VLAN and the GUI binds only there.

If pfSense later feels like more machinery than the job needs, the same hardware runs Debian plus Caddy with a config file about six lines long — the config sketch below. The hardware choice doesn't lock you in.

Picking the proxy

Terminate or pass through? Terminating TLS at the proxy is simpler and lets one wildcard certificate cover everything. Use SNI passthrough only where a backend must present its own cert — an appliance with a pinned certificate, or something you don't control. Mixing both is fine: HAProxy can terminate most hosts and pass through specific ones.

Verified configuration

Checked against current upstream HAProxy and Caddy documentation.

HAProxy — TLS termination with Host-header routing. This is the shape you want for ordinary websites: one certificate set on the frontend, routing by hostname, plain HTTP to the backends.

frontend https_in
    bind *:443 ssl crt /var/etc/haproxy/certs/
    mode http
    acl host_one hdr(host) -i site-one.example.com
    acl host_two hdr(host) -i site-two.example.net
    use_backend bk_one if host_one
    use_backend bk_two if host_two
    default_backend bk_nomatch

backend bk_one
    mode http
    server s1 10.99.10.20:8080 check

HAProxy — SNI passthrough, for the case where a backend must present its own certificate. Routes on the TLS ClientHello without decrypting:

frontend tls_passthrough
    bind *:443
    mode tcp
    tcp-request inspect-delay 5s
    tcp-request content accept if { req.ssl_hello_type 1 }
    use_backend bk_appliance if { req.ssl_sni -i appliance.example.com }
    default_backend bk_terminate
HTTP/2 breaks Host-header routing in TCP mode

HAProxy's documentation is explicit: a TCP-mode frontend "cannot parse HTTP/2 beyond the initial preface," which prevents content-based routing such as selecting a backend based on the Host header. So in passthrough mode you must route on req.ssl_sni — which works fine — and never on hdr(host). If you need Host-header routing, terminate TLS and use mode http. Mixing the two in one config is fine; mixing them in one frontend is not.

Caddy alternative — same job, if pfSense ever feels like more machinery than it's worth:

site-one.example.com {
    reverse_proxy 10.99.10.20:8080
}

site-two.example.net {
    reverse_proxy 10.99.10.31:3000
}

*.example.com {
    tls {
        dns cloudflare {env.CLOUDFLARE_API_TOKEN}
    }
    reverse_proxy 10.99.10.44:80
}
Caddy's DNS challenge needs a custom build

The stock Caddy binary does not include DNS providers. Wildcard certificates via the Cloudflare DNS challenge require building with the provider module (xcaddy build --with github.com/caddy-dns/cloudflare) or using an image that bundles it. Easy to miss, and it fails at certificate issuance rather than at config load.

Two details that will otherwise bite

Internal clients resolving your own domains. Cloudflare returns your public IP, so a laptop inside the house tries to reach your site by going out and back in — NAT hairpin, which is fiddly at best. The clean fix is Firewalla's Custom DNS Rules (Services → Custom DNS Rules), which map a domain straight to an internal IP for LAN clients. Firewalla users run exactly this pattern with local proxy managers. Two caveats: entering a TLD captures its subdomains automatically, and the rules apply globally across all networks — including guest — with no per-VLAN scoping in the UI.

Certificates. Use Let's Encrypt DNS-01 via the Cloudflare API, not HTTP-01. It needs no inbound connectivity at all, so it works identically whether you're on Tunnel or a port forward, and it's the only way to get wildcard certificates.

12 · The spare Gold — revised

The spare is an original Firewalla Gold (gen 1): quad-core Intel 2.2 GHz, 4 GB RAM, 3 Gbps of packet processing — but 4 × 1 GbE ports. That port speed, not the CPU, decides where it belongs.

Don't put it inline as lab edge

Earlier drafts of this plan had the Gold routing the lab behind a transit VLAN. With 1 GbE ports that makes it a 1 Gbps chokepoint between a full rack and everything else — including the UNAS-Pro in the closet. You'd run 10G fiber to the garage and then funnel it through a gigabit port. Moving VM images or backups between lab and NAS would crawl, and you'd be double-NATing to get there.

Better: lab as a VLAN off the Gold Pro

Put the lab on VLAN 99, routed directly by the Gold Pro. That box does 10 Gbps with full IDS/IPS, so inter-VLAN routing runs at line rate: lab↔NAS at 10G, lab↔internet at your full 1000/35, and a single policy plane instead of chained NAT. Isolation is unchanged — Firewalla enforces lab↔house at the VLAN boundary exactly as it would at a separate device, and a rogue DHCP server in the lab stays contained to its own VLAN.

Where the Gold actually earns its keep

Use it as a nested firewall inside the lab — a real device to build test topologies against, break, reconfigure and restore. That's the best home-lab use for it, and it's the one role where 1 GbE ports are completely irrelevant: lab-internal test traffic doesn't need 10G, and having a genuine second firewall to experiment on is worth more than using it as a bottleneck in the production path.

Failing that, keep it as a cold spare for the Gold Pro. It would cap you at 1 Gbps during a failover, which exactly matches your Spectrum downstream anyway — so as an emergency stand-in it costs you nothing but multi-gig LAN routing.

Confirmed inputs

Spectrum GIG, 1000/35. The 35 Mb/s upstream ceiling is real, which is what makes the Nest cameras' ~18 GB/day of outbound video worth eliminating. Cameras: 5 planned, sized for 8 — PoE load is 40 W actual against the Switch SE's 114 W budget, 64 W if you grow into 8. Comfortable either way, and the spare capacity is already accounted for.

13 · Pi-hole

Two Raspberry Pis running Pi-hole. The useful question isn't where to plug them in — it's that Firewalla and Pi-hole both want to own DNS, and per Firewalla's own documentation you cannot fully have both.

The tradeoff, stated plainly

ArchitectureYou gainYou lose
Clients → Pi-hole directly
DHCP hands out the Pi-hole address		Pi-hole sees every client individually — per-client groups, per-client rules, real query logsFirewalla's DNS layer is bypassed entirely. Its own words: DNS "doesn't go through layer 3 of Firewalla. Therefore DNS interception on Firewalla doesn't take effect and DNS-based features don't work"
Clients → Firewalla → Pi-hole upstream
Firewalla's recommendation		Every Firewalla DNS feature keeps working, on top of Pi-hole's blocklistsPi-hole nominally sees one client — the Firewalla — so per-client rules and stats lose meaning. Possibly recoverable, see EDNS0 below
Recommended: Firewalla forwards to Pi-hole

For you this isn't close. You have 282 Firewalla rules, a policy set you've clearly invested in, and a VLAN build ahead that depends on Firewalla enforcing per-network DNS. Trading all of that away for per-client graphs in Pi-hole is the wrong side of the deal. Let Pi-hole do what it's uniquely good at — custom blocklists, regex rules, aggressive filtering — and let Firewalla keep ownership of policy and visibility.

Where they live

Both Pis on the Management VLAN (10). Under the recommended architecture the Firewalla is their only client, which means no other VLAN ever needs a path to them — IoT, Guest, Cameras and DMZ get no route to VLAN 10 at all. Give them static addresses and configure both as upstream resolvers on the Firewalla so one failing doesn't take DNS down.

Keeping two Pi-holes in sync

Use nebula-sync, which targets Pi-hole v6.x specifically and works through Pi-hole's API. It runs in Docker, syncs either everything via Teleporter or selected areas (adlists, domain lists, groups, clients, DNS settings), and supports cron scheduling with webhook notifications. Older guides point at Gravity Sync — the existing Pi runs v6.x (confirmed 24 Aug), and the v6 API change is what prompted the newer tooling.

Don't move Pi-hole into the lab

With a full rack arriving it's tempting to virtualize these. Don't. DNS must not depend on the environment you intend to break. The whole point of the lab is that you can tear it down at will — and the day you do, every device in the house should still resolve names. Two low-power Pis that stay up independently of the rack is exactly the right architecture. Keep them physical, keep them boring.

Three things from the Pi-hole docs worth knowing

Don't install Pi-hole on the Firewalla itself

Firewalla publishes a (beta) guide for running Pi-hole as a Docker container on the Gold. With two Pis already in hand there is no version of this that comes out ahead:

  • It cannot coexist with Firewalla's Family Protect, Adblock or DNS-over-HTTPS — "Firewalla's features always have a higher priority." You'd be disabling the things you're trying to keep.
  • It still loses per-device visibility: with DNS Booster on, every query reaches Pi-hole from 172.16.0.1, so "you will only be able to see localhost and Firewalla on the Pi-Hole portal."
  • It doesn't survive reboots without hand-written startup scripts in post_main.d/.
  • Firewalla recommends a separate USB/SD disk "to avoid overloading Firewalla's internal storage."

So you'd take on fragility in your edge router, lose Firewalla's DNS features and lose per-device stats — the worst of both. Your two Pis are strictly the better answer.

What dedicated Pis actually fix

Most of the objections to Pi-hole on a Firewalla are objections to co-residency, not to the combination. Running them on separate hardware removes nearly all of them.

IssuePi-hole on the FirewallaDedicated Pis

Correction: do not disable DNS Booster

An earlier draft suggested turning DNS Booster off to recover per-client visibility. That was bad advice. Firewalla's documentation warns plainly: "If you turn DNS Booster off, many DNS blocks will NOT work" — some protections combine DNS blocking with IP blocking, and losing the cache undermines them. It is also a per-device setting, not global. Trading away DNS blocking on a device to see its name in Pi-hole's log is the same bad bargain in a smaller package. Leave it on.

Why losing per-client visibility in Pi-hole doesn't matter

You already have per-device DNS visibility — in Firewalla, tied to its device inventory, its 282 rules and the VLANs you're about to build. You don't need it twice. The division of labour is clean: Pi-hole contributes blocklists, regex rules and aggressive filtering; Firewalla owns per-device policy, attribution and enforcement. The one capability you give up is one you already have somewhere better.

Turn on revServers. Formerly called conditional forwarding, it lets Pi-hole resolve local device names when it isn't the DHCP server — it forwards reverse DNS lookups to your DHCP server so reports show hostnames instead of bare IPs. Since the Firewalla is your DHCP authority, point revServers at it with your local CIDR. Without this, every Pi-hole report is a list of IP addresses.

Pi-hole v6 changed authentication. The API is now session-based — POST to /api/auth for a session ID (SID) rather than a static token, with support for application passwords and TOTP. That matters when you configure nebula-sync: generate an application password for it rather than handing it your admin password. If you run Pi-hole in Docker, note also that every v5 environment variable was renamed to an FTLCONF_ equivalent in v6.

Two leaks worth closing

Hardcoded DNS. You already found one: the Orbi resolving via 8.8.8.8, around your Firewalla. That's the same class of problem at scale — plenty of IoT devices ship with hardcoded public resolvers and will ignore whatever DHCP hands them. With ~28 Kasa devices and Nest gear, assume some are doing it. The fix is a Firewalla rule blocking outbound port 53 to anything except your resolvers, so a device that insists on Google DNS simply fails and falls back to the address it was given. Worth doing once the VLANs exist, since you can scope it per-network.

DNS-over-HTTPS. A client using DoH bypasses Pi-hole and Firewalla — it's just HTTPS on 443, invisible to port-53 rules. Browsers increasingly enable it by default. Firewalla can block DoH; turn that on, or the filtering you've built is optional from the client's point of view.
